A consultation request is an ask to talk to a listed attorney or provider — not a booked appointment and not a promise of a response. Understanding the three states it can be in explains what to expect after you <span className="kw">send</span> one.

## A request starts queued

When you send a request, it's marked queued and placed in the provider's own queue — nothing you or the directory does moves it out of that state on its own. There's no case, account, or engagement required to send one; the only information attached is your message and a way to identify who's asking. A queued request isn't a booking and isn't a promise that anyone will respond by a particular time — it's a line item waiting for the one provider it went to.

## Accepting or declining is the provider's call

Nothing in the Marketplace decides for the provider. They review the request and either accept or decline it, and that decision is theirs alone — there's no automatic acceptance, no scoring, and no ranking of requests ahead of others. A declined request stays visible as declined; it isn't deleted or hidden, and sending it doesn't stop you from reaching out to a different listing right away.

## What happens once a request is accepted

Vero keeps a permanent record that the request was accepted: who asked, which listing, which request, and when. That record exists regardless of what happens next, and it doesn't change or disappear later.

What doesn't happen automatically, at least not yet, is any connection between the provider and your case. Vero doesn't add the provider to your case, open a shared workspace, or start a message thread for the two of you when a request is accepted — those connections aren't built yet. An accepted request means the provider agreed to talk to you; everything after that, including how you actually work together, happens outside the Marketplace for now, the same as arranging a first conversation with anyone else you found on your own.

## A request is not the same as a booked meeting

Nothing about the queue holds a calendar slot, sets a time, or schedules a call. If you need an actual appointment time, that gets worked out with the provider once they've accepted your request — the Marketplace only tracks whether they agreed to talk, not when.
