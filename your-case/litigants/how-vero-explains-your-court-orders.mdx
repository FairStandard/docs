A court order is written for the court, not for you. Order analysis takes an order on your case and breaks it into its individual provisions, so you can see what each one actually requires without reading the whole document as a single block of legal language.

## What you'll see

Each provision from the order appears on its own, quoted exactly as the court wrote it, with a plain-language sentence next to it saying what that provision means in practice. Nothing is paraphrased in place of the court's own words — the quote stays, and the plain-language sentence sits alongside it, not instead of it. A custody order's line about "alternating physical custody on a 2-2-3 schedule" gets its own plain-language sentence describing which days that actually means, without dropping the original wording.

Provisions are broken out one at a time rather than left as a single block of text, because a single order can carry a dozen separate obligations — a schedule, a support amount, a set of restrictions — and each one has its own deadline and its own consequence if it's missed. Keeping them separate makes it possible to track each one on its own, instead of re-reading the whole order every time you need to check one detail.

## Reading a provision

Read the plain-language sentence first if you want the short version, then check the quoted text if you want to see exactly how the court phrased it. Both stay on the page together, so you never have to choose between the short version and the source.

If a later order changes or conflicts with an earlier one, both orders and both provisions stay visible — Vero does not hide the earlier order once a new one exists. Seeing both side by side is often the only way to tell exactly what changed and what stayed the same, especially when a modification only touches one or two provisions out of many.

Order analysis explains what an order says. It does not tell you what to do about it, and it does not predict how a judge will rule if a provision is disputed — those are judgment calls, not something a plain-language breakdown can answer for you.

<Note>
  Vero explains legal process. It does not give legal advice.
</Note>
