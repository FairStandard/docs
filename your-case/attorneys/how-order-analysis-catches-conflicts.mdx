An order is written for the court's record, not for quick comparison against another order or a statute. Order analysis breaks every order on a case into its individual provisions, so you can browse and compare them without rereading each order as a single block of text.

## Provisions, not paragraphs

Each provision from an order appears on its own, quoted exactly as the court wrote it, with a plain-language sentence next to it. A single order can carry a dozen separate obligations — a schedule, a support amount, a set of restrictions — and each one has its own deadline and its own consequence if it's missed. Keeping them separate makes each one trackable on its own instead of buried inside the full document.

## Catching conflicts across orders

Because every order on a case is broken into the same kind of provision, you can browse them side by side and catch a conflict — a later order that changes a schedule an earlier one set, or a provision that reads against a statute cited elsewhere on the case. Vero does not hide an earlier order once a new one exists; both stay visible, because seeing them together is often the only way to tell exactly what changed and what stayed the same, especially when a modification touches only one or two provisions out of many.

This matters most on a caseload rather than on a single matter viewed once. A modification filed months after the original order can be easy to miss if you're relying on memory of what the first order said, and a provision that conflicts with a statute cited on the case is easier to catch when both sit in front of you as separate, comparable entries rather than as paragraphs inside two different documents.

## What order analysis doesn't do

Order analysis explains what an order says. It does not tell you or your client what to do about a provision, and it does not predict how a judge will rule if one is disputed — those are judgment calls a plain-language breakdown of the text can't make for you.
