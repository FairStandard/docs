A finding is a statement about what a group of records shows, not a record on its own. Reading the <span className="kw">matrix</span> case by case only works once you know how a finding is built and how patterns relate to it.

## How a finding is built

A finding draws from one or more records — a message, a call recording, a filed document — and states what they show as a plain sentence, with every source record listed next to it, so you can open the source and check the sentence against it yourself. Attribution names who or what the finding concerns. Where the other side has raised a defense against a finding, that defense is attached to it rather than filed separately, so you're never reading a finding without the counter-argument it's already drawn. Each finding also carries a confidence score, reflecting how directly and how consistently the underlying records support the statement.

## How patterns cluster findings

A pattern groups related findings into repeated conduct rather than a single incident — allegations, denials, admissions, and non-responses clustered by claim type. A pattern is only as reliable as the findings inside it: if one finding in a cluster is dismissed after review, the pattern's shape can change, since it's built from the findings that remain rather than a fixed count set at the start.

## Who decides what needs review

A finding's confidence score is a signal, not a decision. Low confidence, an attached defense, or a finding central to a disputed claim are all reasons an item lands in the review queue, where a person — you, on a represented case, or the party themselves on a self-represented one — confirms, flags, or dismisses it. The matrix doesn't resolve a disputed finding on its own; it surfaces the items that need a decision and keeps a record of whoever makes it.
