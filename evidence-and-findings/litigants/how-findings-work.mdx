A finding is Vero's own read of one thing your records show — a sentence, not a paragraph, with the records it came from listed right next to it. Findings do not decide anything for you. They point at what the records already say, and you decide what to do with it.

## What a finding is

Every finding traces back to specific records — a message, a call recording, a document, a note — never to a general impression Vero formed on its own. Some findings are read from a single record: a bank statement showing a transfer that does not match a declared account. Others come from several records read together: a message on Tuesday that contradicts a statement made in a filing three months earlier. Where a call recording has been reviewed, a finding can also include a measured communication trend — a tone or pattern change over time — shown gently and only where you choose to see it. Either way, the records a finding draws from are listed with it, so you can open each one and see for yourself.

## How patterns form

A pattern is several related findings grouped together, because repeated conduct means something different from a single incident. Three missed exchanges on their own might each have an explanation; three missed exchanges that all happened the week before a hearing form a pattern, and Vero groups them and says so in plain language rather than leaving you to notice the grouping yourself. A pattern still shows every finding and every record underneath it — grouping them together does not hide the pieces.

## What is yours to decide

Vero shows you the same findings it would show an attorney. It does not tell you which ones matter most or what to file because of them — that decision, and any judgment about how a finding affects your case, is yours if you're self-represented, or a conversation to have with your attorney if you're represented. A finding marked for review is a flag that something needs a closer, human look before anyone relies on it, not a conclusion Vero has already reached.
