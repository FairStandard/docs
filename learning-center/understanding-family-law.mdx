Family law touches the most consequential decisions of people's lives — who raises the children, how finances are divided, and what protections exist when safety is at risk. Understanding how the law frames these questions is the first step toward navigating your case effectively. This page introduces the core legal concepts you will encounter and explains how FairStandard connects those concepts to the specific statutes and standards that govern your situation.

## Why Family Law Is Complex

A single custody case in Texas draws on more than 7,000 statute sections spread across the Family Code, the Civil Practice and Remedies Code, the Government Code, the Rules of Civil Procedure, and local district rules — and that is before accounting for controlling appellate decisions. The law is not in one place, it is not written for non-lawyers, and it changes. Amendments pass, courts issue new opinions, and local rules get updated without any central announcement.

This fragmentation creates real risk. Litigants who don't know the applicable standard can't evaluate whether an offer is fair. Attorneys who haven't practiced in a jurisdiction recently can miss a rule change that affects strategy. The Learning Center exists to give everyone a reliable starting point — organized by topic, tied to your jurisdiction, and kept current.

## Core Legal Concepts

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Child Custody">
    Custody has two distinct dimensions: **legal custody** and **physical custody**. Legal custody refers to the right to make major decisions about a child's life — education, healthcare, religious upbringing. Physical custody refers to where the child lives and the day-to-day parenting schedule.

    Either dimension can be **joint** (shared between both parents) or **sole** (held by one parent). Joint legal custody is the default in most states; sole physical custody with a structured visitation schedule for the other parent remains common, though true joint physical custody arrangements are increasingly recognized.

    Every custody determination is governed by the **best interests of the child** standard. Courts weigh factors that vary by state but typically include the child's relationship with each parent, each parent's ability to provide stability, the child's adjustment to home and school, any history of domestic violence or substance abuse, and — depending on the child's age — the child's own preferences.

    Custody orders can be **modified** after they are entered, but modification requires showing a material and substantial change in circumstances since the last order was issued. The bar is intentionally high to protect children from repeated litigation.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Child Support">
    Child support is a financial obligation paid by one parent to the other to cover the costs of raising a child. Every state uses a formula, but two primary models exist: the **income shares model** and the **percentage of income model**.

    The **income shares model** — used by the majority of states — calculates support based on both parents' combined income, then assigns each parent a proportional share of the total obligation. The **percentage of income model** — used in states like Texas — calculates support as a fixed percentage of the paying parent's net income, regardless of the receiving parent's earnings.

    Support typically covers housing, food, clothing, healthcare, and education. Many orders separately address extraordinary medical expenses, childcare costs, and extracurricular activities.

    **Enforcement mechanisms** include wage garnishment, license suspension, contempt of court, and — in extreme cases — criminal prosecution. **Modification** is available when there is a significant change in either parent's income or the child's needs, and most states allow a review at regular intervals without requiring proof of changed circumstances.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Division of Property">
    How marital property is divided depends on whether your state follows **community property** or **equitable distribution** rules.

    In **community property states** (including Texas, California, and Arizona), most assets and debts acquired during the marriage are owned equally by both spouses and divided 50/50 on divorce. Property owned before the marriage, or received as a gift or inheritance during the marriage, is generally **separate property** and not subject to division.

    In **equitable distribution states** — the majority — courts divide marital property in a way that is fair but not necessarily equal. Factors include the length of the marriage, each spouse's financial and non-financial contributions, each spouse's earning capacity, and any dissipation of assets.

    **Debt** is treated similarly to assets. Marital debt incurred for joint purposes is generally divisible; pre-marital debt or debt incurred for one spouse's sole benefit may be assigned to that spouse alone. Note that a divorce decree assigns debt between spouses but does not release either party from liability to a creditor — refinancing or paying off joint debt before or during the divorce is often strategically important.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Spousal Support / Alimony">
    Spousal support — also called alimony or spousal maintenance — is a payment from one spouse to the other following divorce. Courts have broad discretion in whether to award it and in what amount and duration.

    The main types are:

    - **Temporary support**: Ordered while the case is pending to maintain the status quo. It ends when final orders are entered.
    - **Rehabilitative support**: Designed to give a lower-earning spouse time and resources to become self-sufficient — through education, job training, or career reentry. It has a defined end date.
    - **Permanent support**: Reserved for long marriages where one spouse is unlikely to achieve self-sufficiency due to age, disability, or the structure of the marriage. It is less common than it once was.

    Factors courts consider include the length of the marriage, each spouse's earning capacity and health, contributions to the other's career or education, the standard of living during the marriage, and any history of family violence.

    Most support awards are **modifiable** on a showing of changed circumstances and terminate automatically on the recipient's remarriage or — in many states — cohabitation.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Protective Orders">
    A protective order is a court order that restricts one person's contact with or proximity to another. In family law, protective orders are most commonly sought in cases involving domestic violence, stalking, or harassment.

    The process typically has two stages. First, a party can seek a **temporary ex parte order** — issued by a judge without notice to the other side — based on a sworn statement showing immediate danger. This order takes effect immediately and typically lasts until a full hearing can be held, usually within 14 to 21 days depending on the jurisdiction.

    At the **full hearing**, both parties have the opportunity to present evidence. If the court finds that family violence occurred and is likely to occur in the future, it issues a **final protective order**, which can last up to two years or longer in cases involving serious violence.

    Violating a protective order is a criminal offense. FairStandard tracks order compliance and flags documented violations as part of your case evidence, ensuring that any breach is recorded and ready to present to the court.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
