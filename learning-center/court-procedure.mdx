Knowing what the law says about custody or property is only half the picture. The other half is procedure — the rules that govern how you get your case in front of a judge, what happens at each stage, and what the court expects from you along the way. Procedural missteps can be just as damaging as weak facts, and in some cases more so. This page walks you through the life of a family law case from the first filing to the final order, and explains how FairStandard keeps you on the right side of every deadline and requirement.

## The Stages of a Family Law Case

<Steps>
  <Step title="Filing the Petition">
    One party — the petitioner — starts the case by filing an original petition with the district or family court. The petition identifies the parties, states the relief being requested (custody, support, property division, divorce, protective orders), and is filed with the clerk along with the required filing fee. Filing the petition starts the clock on response deadlines for the other side and triggers the case number that all future filings will carry.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Service of Process">
    The other party — the respondent — must be formally served with a copy of the petition and a citation issued by the court. Service must be completed in a legally recognized way: personal delivery by a process server, certified mail where permitted, or substitute service authorized by the court. The case cannot move forward until service is complete. FairStandard tracks this deadline and flags it in your Next Steps panel so it doesn't stall your case.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Temporary Orders Hearing">
    Early in most contested cases, either party can request a temporary orders hearing. The court uses this hearing to establish interim arrangements — where the children will live, how much support will be paid, who stays in the family home, and what access each party has to joint accounts and property — while the full case proceeds. Temporary orders are binding and enforceable immediately. They often set the practical baseline that final orders build from, which makes this hearing strategically significant.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Discovery">
    Discovery is the formal process by which both sides exchange information and evidence. It includes written interrogatories (questions that must be answered under oath), requests for production of documents (financial records, communications, business records), requests for admissions, and depositions (sworn testimony taken outside of court). Discovery is where hidden assets get uncovered, inconsistencies get locked in, and the true factual record gets built. FairStandard's evidence vault tracks what has been requested, what has been produced, and what is still outstanding — so nothing gets buried or overlooked.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Mediation (Where Required)">
    Many jurisdictions require the parties to attempt mediation before the court will set a final hearing. Mediation is a structured negotiation facilitated by a neutral third party. It is confidential, and nothing said in mediation can be used as evidence. If the parties reach an agreement, it is reduced to writing and submitted to the court for approval. If mediation fails, the case proceeds to a final hearing. The FairStandard Marketplace can help you find a qualified mediator in your jurisdiction.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Final Hearing or Trial">
    If the parties have not reached a full agreement, the court holds a final hearing or trial. Both sides present evidence, call witnesses, and make legal arguments. The judge — family law cases almost never go to a jury — evaluates the evidence against the applicable legal standards and issues rulings on every contested issue: custody, support, property, debt, and any other relief that was requested. Preparation for the final hearing is where FairStandard's evidence organization and law assembly are most directly useful.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Final Orders & Enforcement">
    After the hearing, the court signs final orders that resolve all contested issues. These orders are entered into the court's record and become immediately enforceable. Violating a final order — failing to pay support, interfering with a custody schedule, hiding or dissipating assets in violation of a property order — can result in contempt of court, fines, or jail. FairStandard tracks compliance with orders after they are entered and flags documented violations so that enforcement action can be taken quickly and with a complete evidentiary record.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Filings and Formats

Every jurisdiction has its own requirements for how court documents must be prepared, formatted, and submitted. Local rules specify margin sizes, font requirements, caption formats, how exhibits must be labeled, whether electronic filing is required or optional, and what cover sheets or verification pages must be included. County-level rules sometimes layer additional requirements on top of state rules. Getting the format wrong can result in a clerk rejecting your filing — which means your document was never filed, even if you submitted it on time.

## How to Avoid Losing on Procedure

The merits of your case — your evidence, your legal arguments, the facts on your side — can only help you if you stay in the game procedurally. Courts are not forgiving of procedural failures, and they do not typically make exceptions because a party didn't know the rule.

<Warning>
  Missing a filing deadline can result in default judgments, waived rights, or dismissal. Always confirm critical dates with the court clerk or your attorney.
</Warning>

Common procedural pitfalls include: missing the deadline to respond to a petition (which can result in a default judgment against you), failing to properly serve the other party, submitting discovery responses late or incompletely, missing the deadline to designate expert witnesses, and failing to comply with local formatting rules that result in rejected filings.
